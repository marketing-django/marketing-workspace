# Nonprofit Brand Messaging

## When to Use This Skill

Use this skill to establish or fix the foundational messaging layer that every other communications
channel should draw from: voice and tone, positioning relative to peer organizations, and a
structured messaging house. Typical triggers: a rebrand or brand refresh, inconsistent tone across
channels reported by staff or a board member, a merger requiring unified messaging
(pairs with `nonprofit-mergers-fiscal-sponsorship`), a new ED wanting to reset public narrative, or
onboarding new staff/volunteers who need a messaging reference.

**Boundary:** This skill produces the reusable *messaging foundation* (voice, positioning, pillar
messages, boilerplate) — not channel-specific execution. Individual beneficiary narratives are
`nonprofit-storytelling`; annual report content structure is `nonprofit-annual-report`; social
calendars/captions are `nonprofit-social-media`; press materials are `nonprofit-media-relations`;
donation page copy is `nonprofit-donation-page-copy`. Those skills should cite this skill's messaging
house as their source of truth for word choice and positioning, not redefine it.

## Standard Deliverable: The Messaging House

Structure messaging as a "house" — a standard framework (roof, pillars, foundation) that keeps every
channel's language traceable to one source of truth:

- **Foundation:** Mission statement, vision statement, and 1-2 sentence "elevator pitch" (what we do,
  who we serve, why it matters, in plain language a stranger understands in 10 seconds).
- **Roof (Positioning statement):** One paragraph stating who you serve, what makes your approach
  distinct from peer/adjacent organizations, and the change you exist to create. Use the classic
  positioning template as a drafting aid (don't publish the template language verbatim): "For
  [audience], [org] is the [category] that [key differentiator], because [reason to believe]."
- **Pillars (3-4 key messages):** Each pillar is a short claim + a proof point (a stat, program
  result, or third-party validation) + supporting language. Every channel's content should map back
  to one of these pillars — if a piece of content doesn't support any pillar, question why it's being
  published.
- **Voice and tone guide:** Define voice as fixed personality traits (e.g., "direct, warm, no jargon,
  never alarmist") that don't change, versus tone as the register that flexes by context (a crisis
  update is more measured; a volunteer recruitment post is more energetic) — write 3-5 voice
  adjectives, each with a "we sound like this / not like this" pair of example sentences.
- **Boilerplate:** A standard 2-4 sentence "About [Org]" paragraph for press releases, grant cover
  pages, and website footers, reviewed/updated at most annually so it doesn't drift out of sync with
  actual program scope.
- **Lexicon/terminology rules:** Approved and disallowed terms (e.g., "people experiencing
  homelessness" not "the homeless"; program-specific terms of art defined once) so language is
  consistent and identity-respectful across every writer.

## Step-by-Step: Building or Refreshing Messaging

1. Audit current materials (website, last 10 social posts, most recent appeal, annual report) for
   voice consistency and positioning drift — note where language contradicts itself across channels.
2. Interview 5-8 stakeholders across roles (ED, program staff, board member, a donor, a beneficiary if
   appropriate) with a short structured protocol: "In your own words, what do we do and for whom?"
   "What makes us different from [named peer org]?" Divergent answers reveal where positioning is
   unclear internally, which always precedes external inconsistency.
3. Draft the positioning statement first — it's the hardest-fought sentence and everything else
   derives from it. Pressure-test it against 2-3 real peer/competitor organizations' positioning to
   confirm genuine differentiation, not generic mission language interchangeable with any nonprofit.
4. Draft 3-4 pillar messages, each traceable to a real program outcome or proof point — reject any
   pillar that can't be backed by a specific fact within 30 seconds.
5. Write the voice/tone guide with contrastive examples (right/wrong sentence pairs), not just
   adjectives — adjectives alone ("authentic," "passionate") don't change agent or staff writing
   behavior; example pairs do.
6. Draft boilerplate and lexicon list; circulate to program and development staff for terminology
   accuracy (program staff often catch outdated or non-preferred population terminology).
7. Package as a one-page (or short PDF) messaging guide staff can actually reference — a 40-page brand
   book with no summary page will not get used day-to-day.
8. Roll out with a short training/walkthrough for staff and key volunteers, plus a note on where the
   guide lives and who owns updates.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Positioning that's actually generic mission language** — "we empower communities to thrive"
  could describe almost any nonprofit; test every draft positioning statement by asking whether a
  named peer organization could say the exact same sentence.
- **Voice defined only in adjectives**, producing no actual change in how staff write — always pair
  adjectives with example sentences.
- **Messaging house built in isolation from program/finance reality** — pillar messages must be
  provable; a pillar claiming "we serve the whole family" is a liability if only one program actually
  does.
- **No update cadence** — org grows or pivots programs and the boilerplate/pillars silently go stale;
  set an annual review trigger (tie to annual report production cycle).
- **Treating this as a graphic design project** — logo/color/font decisions are visual identity, not
  messaging; a messaging house can and should be drafted before or independent of a visual rebrand.
- **Skipping internal alignment** before external rollout — if the ED and board chair describe the
  mission differently in the same donor meeting, no channel-level fix will resolve the inconsistency.

## Practitioner vs. Advisor Framing

- **As the practitioner** (communications/ED staff): use the stakeholder interview step to surface
  internal disagreement before writing anything, and ship the one-page guide, not just a strategy
  deck — adoption depends on staff actually being able to find and use it.
- **As an advisor/consultant**: treat divergent stakeholder answers to "what do we do and for whom"
  as a diagnostic finding worth reporting to the board directly — inconsistent internal understanding
  of positioning is often a governance/strategy symptom (pairs with `nonprofit-strategic-planning`),
  not merely a communications polish issue.
